Mission briefing

A stranger's eye on your forms.

The scanner visits the page from our server, exactly as a member of the public would. It does not log in, submit forms or guess passwords. It reads what is already public and compares it with the habits Act 843 expects.

01

Recon · find the doors

Every form and field is read. Fields that collect names, phone and MoMo numbers, Ghana Card, date of birth, addresses or payment details are flagged.

02

Probe · check the locks

HTTPS, where forms send their data, browser security headers, tracking scripts, a privacy notice, consent boxes and who is responsible.

03

Report · plain words

Each finding is tied to one of the eight principles in section 17, with what it means for real people and a fix you can hand to your web designer.

The yardstick

Eight principles. Section 17.

Every check is filed under one of them. Each counts as pass (1), check (0.5) or fix now (0), weighted by how much it matters: 80 and above is a strong start, 50 to 79 needs work, below 50 is at risk.

Field note

"Checking for empty fields in JavaScript improves the user's experience, but it is not security."

A lesson from my industrial attachment with Accolm, an IT security company. Accolm Check applies the same idea to whole websites: what the browser shows is only half the story.

Section 19 of Act 843: personal data may only be processed if the purpose is necessary, relevant and not excessive.

Rules of engagement

Automated first-pass screening, not legal advice. It reads only what any visitor can see: the page and, where linked, its privacy page. It cannot see how data is stored, who can access it, or whether the business is on the Data Protection Register, so it cannot confirm that a website complies with the Act. For an authoritative position, consult the Data Protection Commission or a qualified lawyer.
